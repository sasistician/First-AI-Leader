Recap & Bridge · From Week 2 02 — 24
before today

You already grounded Claude in what your project is. That's not the same as what you do to it.

Last week you gave Ledger a CLAUDE.md, a first memory entry, and a pointer to its own docs — durable facts about the project that persist across every session. That's grounding, and it worked: the same question, asked before and after, got an unrecognizably better answer the second time. But grounding is fact-shaped. It answers "what's true about this codebase." It doesn't answer "here's the five-step procedure we follow every time we do X" — that's process-shaped, and nothing from last week packages it.

  • CLAUDE.md and memory hold static or slow-changing facts — conventions, decisions, pointers to where the real answer lives.
  • A recurring task is a procedure — a sequence of judgment calls, not a fact you can state once and be done.
  • Every time that task comes up, someone re-explains the procedure from scratch, in the prompt, from memory, hoping they remember every step.

Grounding solved what Claude knows about your world. It never solved the thing you keep re-typing.

The Problem · Part 1 03 — 24
a real project, not a hypothetical

Three engineers, one Ledger refund fix, three different procedures.

Ledger — grounded last week — now needs a new refund reason code. That's a five-step procedure: check idempotency-key handling, append an entry to the audit log in the house format, publish to the billing-events topic, extend the refund-state enum, and add an integration test against the sandbox payment provider. It's well understood. It's even written down, in a wiki page nobody opens mid-sprint. But it isn't a fact about Ledger — it's a thing you do to Ledger — so it never made it into CLAUDE.md, and three different engineers each explain it to Claude fresh, in their own words, from memory.

  • Engineer A writes a careful five-step prompt. Ships clean.
  • Engineer B remembers four of the five steps. Ships without the sandbox integration test — nobody notices until the sandbox contract changes.
  • Engineer C doesn't know the procedure exists at all, and asks Claude to "just add a refund code." Claude does something reasonable. It's still wrong.

The procedure was never the hard part. Getting it applied the same way, every time, without anyone re-typing it, is.

What Is a Skill? · Part 1 04 — 24
the missing packaging layer

A Skill is a procedure Claude can find, load, and follow — on its own.

Concretely: a folder with a SKILL.md file — frontmatter (name + description) plus the steps — and, optionally, bundled scripts or reference docs. Claude reads your current task, matches it against every Skill's description, and if one fits, loads it and follows it. Nobody re-explains the refund-code procedure next time; it's a file, and Claude goes and gets it.

  • It's a file, not a habit. Versioned, reviewable, and shareable like any other code — not something that lives only in one engineer's prompt history.
  • It's discoverable, not injected. Claude decides relevance from the description; an unrelated task never loads it, and never pays its context cost.
  • It's judgment, not a macro. Claude reads and follows the steps, adapting them to the situation at hand — the way a competent engineer follows a runbook rather than executing it byte-for-byte.

Same move as pulling a duplicated block out into a well-named function — except the caller deciding when to invoke it is Claude, using judgment rather than a fixed condition, and the steps themselves get adapted each call, not replayed byte-for-byte the way a function's would be.

The Spectrum · Part 1 05 — 24
four ways to hand off a procedure

Prompt, script, Skill, subagent — same goal, four different trade-offs.

Before building one, it's worth being precise about what a Skill actually replaces — and what it doesn't.

01Prompt
What it is

A one-off natural-language instruction, typed fresh or copy-pasted from a doc, a Slack thread, or your own memory.

Where the reuse actually comes from

None — you are the packaging. If you forget a step, or you're not the one prompting, the procedure doesn't happen.

02Script
What it is

Deterministic code that executes a fixed procedure with zero judgment.

Where the reuse actually comes from

High for the exact case it was written for, zero the moment the task varies — and you still have to know it exists and run it yourself.

03Skill
What it is

A packaged procedure (SKILL.md plus optional scripts/resources) that Claude discovers and follows using judgment.

Where the reuse actually comes from

Written once, fires automatically whenever a task matches its description — across sessions, projects, and teammates.

04Subagent
What it is

A separate, delegated agent invocation with its own context and tools, dispatched to do a bounded piece of work and report back.

Where the reuse actually comes from

The agent type is reusable (e.g. "code-reviewer"), but each call is a fresh, isolated unit of delegated work — not a shared recipe.

Notice the pattern: reusability climbs from prompt to Skill. But "more autonomous" isn't the same axis as "more reusable" — conflating them is where this gets misused. More on the next slide.

The Spectrum · Part 2 06 — 24
autonomy and reuse are not the same axis

Skills and subagents both use judgment. They differ on delegation, not on how much judgment gets applied.

The natural next question: isn't a Skill just a small subagent? No. A Skill runs inside your current agent, in your current session — it temporarily extends what that agent knows how to do, then the session continues exactly where it left off. A subagent is a different invocation entirely: its own context window, its own tool access, and it reports back rather than folding into your conversation. Use a Skill when you want the agent you're already talking to to do the task, just following a known-good procedure. Use a subagent when you want to isolate the work — a separate context budget, something parallelizable, or something you'd rather not have clutter the main session.

1 Prompt Reuse lives in your memory. Forget it, lose it.
→
2 Script Reuse lives in fixed code. Reliable, until the task varies at all.
→
3 Skill Reuse lives in a discoverable procedure Claude finds and follows itself.
→
4 Subagent Reuse lives in a delegated role, not a shared recipe — a different axis, not a step further along this one.

For this week, the boundary that matters is prompt vs. script vs. Skill. Subagents get their own week — for now, just don't mistake "runs on its own" for "more capable."

Anatomy of a Skill · Part 2 07 — 24
two fields decide whether a skill fires at all

The frontmatter — name and description — is the entire discovery surface. Everything else in the file is invisible until that matches.

SKILL.md opens with YAML frontmatter — two fields, name and description, and nothing else in that block. That's not an accident of format: it's the only part of the file Claude reads before deciding whether the Skill is even relevant. Every installed Skill's frontmatter gets scanned against your current task, every time, regardless of whether it ends up used. The steps underneath aren't loaded until a match happens. Write a vague description and a genuinely useful Skill never fires; write a precise one and it fires exactly when it should, and stays silent otherwise.

illustrative, not a real file 
---
name: ledger-refund-code
description: Add a new refund reason code to Ledger — idempotency check,
  audit-log entry, billing-events publish, refund-state enum extension, and a
  sandbox integration test. Use when adding or changing refund reason codes.
---
  • name — a short slug. Identifies the Skill in logs and lets you refer to it directly — description does the matching heavy-lifting; name is a short identifier that mirrors it.
  • description — the only field Claude uses to judge relevance. It has to say both what the Skill does and when to use it — a title alone won't trigger a match on the right task.
  • Everything below the frontmatter — steps, bundled scripts, reference docs — is dead weight to the matching step. None of it gets read until description has already done its job.

A Skill with a weak description is functionally no Skill at all — it's a folder Claude will never open.

Anatomy of a Skill · Part 2 08 — 24
why skill.md stays thin

SKILL.md is an index, not a reference manual. Detail lives one hop away, loaded only when the procedure actually needs it.

Once a Skill matches, its SKILL.md loads into context — but that doesn't mean everything it might ever need should live inline. A Skill that packages a genuinely detailed procedure — a full design system, a set of code templates, a style guide — could bloat SKILL.md into a wall of text Claude has to hold on every single invocation, most of it irrelevant to today's task. The fix is the same discipline as splitting a bloated module into an index plus focused files: SKILL.md stays a short set of steps and pointers; the heavy reference material lives in separate files that only get read when a specific step actually calls for them.

  • Frontmatter loads for every installed Skill, every time a task comes in — it has to stay cheap.
  • SKILL.md's body loads once a match happens — still kept light: steps and pointers, not the reference material itself.
  • Bundled resource files load only when the step in progress actually needs that detail — not all at once, and not on every invocation.

Same instinct as a lazy import: load what the current line needs, not the whole library, just in case.

Building Your Own · Part 3 · Step 1 of 4 12 — 24
step 1 of 4 · before you write a line of skill.md

Not every recurring task is skill-shaped. Run the candidate against this checklist first.

Building a Skill is real work: a description that has to be precise, steps that have to generalize, and — often — resource files to structure. Spend that effort on the wrong candidate and you get one of two failures: a script's job wearing a Skill's overhead, or a task so fuzzy no description will ever fire on it reliably. The refund-reason-code procedure from Part 1 clears every check below. That's not a coincidence — it's exactly why it's the example this course keeps coming back to.

  • It recurs, and not just for you. If you're the only person who will ever hit this task, a good one-off prompt is cheaper than a Skill.
  • It's a sequence of steps, not a single fact. A fact — "the audit log uses house format X" — belongs in CLAUDE.md, not a Skill. Skills exist for procedures with more than one step.
  • It needs judgment applied to those steps, not identical mechanical execution. If every input produces the exact same output with zero adaptation, write a script — it'll be more reliable on that exact case than a Skill (see the comparison table, slide 5). A Skill earns its place when the steps have to be read and adapted to what's actually in front of you.
  • You can state it in one sentence that names both what it is and when to use it. If you're struggling to summarize the task that crisply, you don't understand the pattern well enough to package it yet — or it's quietly two tasks sharing one name. (Next slide is entirely about this.)
  • Getting it wrong has a real, noticeable cost. If nobody would care whether a step got skipped, it isn't worth packaging.

Five checks, one candidate: Ledger's refund-code procedure clears all five. Clear four or five and it's worth building. Clear one or two and you've usually found a script, a CLAUDE.md fact, or a task that isn't well-understood enough to package yet.

Building Your Own · Part 3 · Step 2 of 4 13 — 24
step 2 of 4 · the only line claude reads before deciding

Write the description before you write a single step — it's the only part of the file that decides whether any of the rest ever runs.

Slide 7 already established that description is the entire discovery surface. Knowing that doesn't make writing one easy — most bad descriptions fail in one of two specific ways, and neither looks broken until you watch it in use. Below: the same ledger-refund-code Skill, described three different ways.

Description

"Handles refund codes."

Result

Never fires.

Why

Generic verb, no trigger condition, no distinguishing detail. It doesn't describe anything specific enough to match against — so it matches nothing.

Description

"Updates Ledger's billing system."

Result

Misfires.

Why

Scope too wide. It also reads as a match for unrelated billing tasks — a pricing change, a billing-dashboard fix — that need none of the five steps. A Skill that fires on the wrong task is as broken as one that never fires.

Description
callback · slide 7's illustrative frontmatter, verbatim

"Add a new refund reason code to Ledger — idempotency check, audit-log entry, billing-events publish, refund-state enum extension, and a sandbox integration test. Use when adding or changing refund reason codes."

Result

Fires reliably.

Why

States both the what (the five concrete steps) and the when (the exact phrase — "adding or changing refund reason codes" — not "billing" in general). This is slide 7's illustrative frontmatter, verbatim.

  • Use the words your team actually says when they ask for this, not a paraphrase of your own intent — Claude matches against the request as typed, not against what you meant.
  • State the "when" explicitly. The single most common way a good Skill never fires is a description that only says what it does and leaves the trigger condition implicit.
  • Write the boundary as deliberately as the content — a description should exclude neighboring tasks, not just include the target one.
  • Test it against 2-3 adjacent tasks before you trust it: would this description also plausibly match on something it shouldn't?

A description is a contract with Claude's matching pass, not a comment for a human reader — write it the way you'd write a docstring that decides whether the function underneath it ever runs at all.

Building Your Own · Part 3 · Step 3 of 4 14 — 24
step 3 of 4 · keep the index thin

Once the description fires, don't undo the win by making SKILL.md itself a wall of text.

This is the same discipline from slide 8, now as a build step rather than a principle. frontend-slides is the proof it holds at real scale: SKILL.md stays a 380-line index of steps and named principles; the 939 combined lines of STYLE_PRESETS.md, viewport-base.css, html-template.md, and animation-patterns.md — the Supporting Files table from two slides back — sit outside it, each read only in the phase that needs it. Building your own Skill means making that same call the moment a step in your procedure needs more than a few lines of reference material.

  • Start by listing your procedure's steps as a short numbered list in SKILL.md. That list is the index — not the place to also carry a schema, a full style guide, or a long enum.
  • The moment a step needs more than a few lines of reference material, split it into its own file — exactly the move frontend-slides made putting style choices in STYLE_PRESETS.md and HTML boilerplate in html-template.md, instead of inlining either into SKILL.md.
  • Reference each split-out file from SKILL.md the way frontend-slides does in its own Supporting Files table: name it, state its purpose, state which step reads it.
  • Not everything belongs in a resource file — if a piece of content is needed on every single invocation regardless of path, inline it. Progressive disclosure only pays off for material that's conditional on which branch of the procedure gets taken.

SKILL.md is the index; the resource files are the stacks. frontend-slides proves the split holds up at 1,300+ combined lines — the same discipline scales down just as cleanly to a five-step refund-code procedure with one reference file.

Building Your Own · Part 3 · Step 4 of 4 15 — 24
step 4 of 4 · a skill.md is a folder until it's proven

An untested Skill hasn't earned the name yet — it's a folder that might fire.

Writing SKILL.md doesn't mean it works. "Testing a Skill" isn't a unit test — it's running it against real, unscripted requests, phrased the way your team actually talks, and watching what happens at both ends: did it fire when it should have, and did it stay silent when it shouldn't have.

  • Run it on the real task, phrased naturally. Ask for the change the way Engineer C would — "just add a refund code" — not the way you phrased the description. If it doesn't fire on natural phrasing, the description doesn't match reality yet.
  • Check whether it fired at all. Silence is a description failure — go back to slide 13's rules: is the "when" explicit? Does it use the words people actually use?
  • Check whether it fired on the wrong task. Try 2-3 adjacent tasks that share vocabulary — a general billing fix, an unrelated audit-log change — and confirm it stays silent on all of them.
  • Watch what it does once loaded, not just whether it loaded. Did it follow the steps in order? Did it reach for resource files at the right phase, or load everything upfront? A vague step is where it improvises wrong.
  • Tighten and re-run — don't rewrite from scratch. Most fixes are narrow: add the missing trigger phrase, split out a stray resource file, sharpen one ambiguous step. Then run the same checks again.

Ship a Skill the way you'd ship any other code that decides when to run itself: prove it fires when it should, stays silent when it shouldn't, and does the right thing — then let your team depend on it.

Discovery & Invocation · Part 4 · 1 of 4 16 — 24
one task, every installed skill, one matching pass

Discovery isn't a lookup against one Skill. It's a judgment call made across every installed Skill at once.

Slide 7 established that every installed Skill's frontmatter gets scanned against your current task. What that actually looks like in practice: there's no priority order, no "first match wins," no alphabetical tiebreak, no most-recently-installed bonus. Claude reads your task once and weighs it against every candidate description at the same time — the same kind of relevance judgment it applies to interpreting the request itself, not a fixed router with hard-coded rules. When exactly one description clearly fits, that one fires and the rest stay silent. The harder case is when more than one plausibly could.

  • It's a field, not a queue. All installed Skills are candidates simultaneously — the question Claude is actually answering is "which of these fits best," not "does this one fit, yes or no."
  • Specificity is the tiebreaker — the same axis from slide 13. If two Skills' descriptions both plausibly cover a "billing" task, the one that names the exact trigger phrase — "refund reason codes," not "billing system" — wins the near-tie, not by rule, but by being the better-worded match.
  • A vague description doesn't fail safely — it loses fights it didn't know it was in. It's not just "might never fire" (slide 13's first failure mode); against a sibling Skill with a sharper description on an overlapping task, it can lose the match entirely, silently, with no error to notice.
  • A genuine tie is a design smell, not a Claude bug. If two Skills' descriptions are equally specific to the same task, that's a sign their scopes actually overlap and should be split or merged — not something to work around with cleverer phrasing.

Writing a precise description was never just about making your one Skill fire. It's about making it win — against every other candidate sitting in the same room.

Discovery & Invocation · Part 4 · 2 of 4 17 — 24
firing a skill isn't free — but staying installed is

A Skill's cost is paid once, at match time. CLAUDE.md's cost is paid every session, whether today's task needs it or not.

Slide 8 already established that once a Skill matches, its SKILL.md body loads into context — that's real token cost, on top of the frontmatter scan every installed Skill already pays every time (slide 7). Set that against CLAUDE.md, which W2 grounded Ledger in: CLAUDE.md loads in full, every session, unconditionally — whether today's task touches billing, refunds, or nothing about Ledger at all. That's the whole distinction. CLAUDE.md is always-on context; a Skill's body is conditional context, gated behind a match that may never come.

  • CLAUDE.md: always-on. Loads at session start, every session, in full, regardless of relevance to what you're about to ask.
  • Skill frontmatter: cheap and constant. name + description for every installed Skill gets scanned every time — small, fixed, and paid whether or not it matches.
  • Skill body: conditional and deferred. The steps underneath only load if the description matched this task. Zero cost on every task that isn't this one — no matter how many Skills are sitting installed.
  • The practical payoff: you can install a Skill for every team's recurring runbook — refunds, deploys, incident response, all of it — without inflating the context budget of a session that touches none of them. Installed isn't the same as loaded.

CLAUDE.md is the tax you pay for grounding, every session, no exceptions. A Skill is closer to a tool on a shelf — it only costs you anything the moment you actually reach for it.

Discovery & Invocation · Part 4 · 3 of 4 18 — 24
four layers, one project, four different questions

SKILL.md, CLAUDE.md, memory, and RAG aren't competing options — they're four different answers to four different questions.

W2 gave Ledger three grounding layers. This week added a fourth. Laid side by side, each one answers a question the others don't.

CLAUDE.md
What It Holds

Durable, slow-changing project facts — conventions, architecture, pointers to where the real answer lives (e.g., the billing-events topic name, the house audit-log format).

When It Loads & How It's Found

Loads in full, every session, unconditionally. Not discovered — just always there.

Memory
What It Holds

A record of specific past decisions and feedback, tied to when and why they happened — organized into the taxonomy from W2 (User / Feedback / Project / Reference).

When It Loads & How It's Found

Surfaced selectively, by relevance to the current task — not dumped in full like CLAUDE.md, and not scanned like a Skill's frontmatter; the relevant entries get pulled in when the topic recurs.

RAG
What It Holds

Whatever's too large or too volatile to keep in CLAUDE.md at all — full wiki pages, past PRs, long spec docs — retrieved from the underlying corpus at query time.

When It Loads & How It's Found

Nothing loads by default. A query at the moment of need retrieves the specific matching chunk — the rest of the corpus stays untouched.

Skill (SKILL.md)
What It Holds

A procedure — a sequence of steps requiring judgment to apply, not a fact and not a corpus (slides 4, 12).

When It Loads & How It's Found

Frontmatter scanned every session, cheaply, against every task (slide 7). Full body loads only on a match (slides 8, 17) — otherwise it costs nothing at all.

These compose on a single task rather than substitute for each other. Take the refund-code change from Part 1 again: CLAUDE.md supplies the fact that billing-events is the topic to publish to; memory supplies the Project-category record of why the sandbox integration test became mandatory (a past incident, not a rule anyone would think to restate); RAG pulls the exact audit-log field spec from a wiki page too long to keep inline in CLAUDE.md; and the ledger-refund-code Skill supplies the procedure itself — the ordered steps that turn all three of those facts into a finished change. No single layer does this task alone.

Same question as W2's "what's true about this codebase" versus this week's "what do you do to it" — just now with four layers instead of one, each loaded on its own terms, each discovered its own way.

Discovery & Invocation · Part 4 · 4 of 4 19 — 24
the pattern generalizes past this course's two examples

Ledger's refund codes and frontend-slides are two data points. Here are four more, from four different corners of the job.

Run slide 12's checklist against any of these and they clear it the same way the refund-code procedure did: they recur across people, they're a sequence requiring judgment rather than a single fact or a fixed script, they compress into one honest sentence, and skipping a step has a real cost that shows up later, not immediately.

  • A deploy runbook — canary rollout, health-check thresholds, rollback trigger. Recurs every release, across whoever's on point that week; "roll back if error rate exceeds baseline by X for Y minutes" is a judgment call against live data, not a mechanical check — exactly why it's not just a script.
  • A code-review checklist tailored to your team's actual failure modes — missing feature-flag guards, unhandled migration rollback, absent test for the exact edge case that bit you last quarter. Generic review advice is a fact ("we require tests"); your team's specific, hard-won checklist is a procedure worth packaging precisely because it was earned the expensive way.
  • A commit-message / PR-description convention — ticket-ID linking, breaking-change flagging, the specific sections a reviewer expects to find. Easy to state as one sentence, easy to skip under deadline pressure, and the cost — an unreviewable PR, an unlinked ticket — shows up in someone else's sprint, not yours.
  • An incident-response procedure — who gets paged, what gets captured in the timeline while memory is fresh, what a blameless postmortem template requires before it's considered done. The highest-stakes case on this list: skipped under pressure precisely when it matters most, which is exactly the argument for packaging it as something Claude follows rather than something an on-call engineer has to remember cold at 3 a.m.

None of these are exotic. They're the runbooks every engineering org already has, half- written, in a wiki nobody opens mid-incident — which was exactly Ledger's refund-code problem, four teams over.

this week's lab · a preview, not a build spec

This week: a real chatbot — guardrails, a judge, memory, a router, a UI. One recurring piece of it becomes a Skill.

This week's lab is a working chatbot, built end-to-end: input/output guardrails, an LLM-as-judge step for quality control, persistent memory, a router that switches between models depending on the task, a Gradio front end, and uv managing the environment underneath all of it. It's not a toy — it's a system with enough recurring, judgment-heavy procedure inside it that at least one piece of it is worth packaging the way this entire lecture has been about: as a Skill, not something re-explained fresh every time you touch it. Full build details land with the lab guide, not here — this is a preview, not a spec.

  • Guardrails — checks the chatbot runs before a response is considered safe to return.
  • LLM-as-judge — a model call whose job is evaluating another model's output, not generating the user-facing answer.
  • Memory — persisted state the chatbot draws on across turns and sessions, not just within one conversation.
  • Router — logic that decides which model handles a given request, instead of sending everything to one model by default.
  • Gradio — the UI layer end users actually interact with.
  • uv — how the project's Python environment gets managed and reproduced.

Start thinking now about which piece of this — a guardrail check, the judge's rubric, the router's decision logic — is a procedure you'll want Claude to follow the same way twice. That's the one you'll package as a Skill.

Apply · Map Your Runbooks · 2 of 2 21 — 24
apply · homework before next session

Find one or two of your own team's runbooks that are skill-shaped — and prove it, don't assume it.

Slide 19 gave four examples from other people's jobs. This one is about yours. Somewhere in your team's process there's a procedure that gets re-explained instead of packaged — pull it out of the wiki page nobody opens mid-sprint and run it through slide 12's checklist for real, not from memory.

  • List 3-5 candidates first, not one. Deploys, on-call handoffs, code review, release notes, incident postmortems, onboarding a service to CI — whatever your team actually repeats.
  • Run each one through slide 12's five checks: does it recur across people, is it a sequence rather than a single fact, does it need judgment rather than identical mechanical execution, can you state it in one sentence, and does skipping it cost something real.
  • Pick the 1-2 that clear four or five checks. The rest aren't wasted — they're now correctly identified as scripts, CLAUDE.md facts, or tasks nobody's understood well enough to package yet.
  • Write the one-sentence description before anything else — the same discipline as slide 13. If you can't state what it is and when to use it that cleanly, you've found the actual gap, not a checklist failure.
  • Bring your 1-2 candidates to next session, ready to talk through where the description almost misfires.

The checklist was never the point of slide 12 — finding out which of your team's tribal knowledge is actually worth packaging is. This is where you find out.

Three questions to sit with before next session.

Not a quiz — there's no graded answer to any of these. They're meant to seed the discussion at the start of next session, so come with a specific answer, not a general one.

three prompts to answer out loud
  • Think about the last time a recurring procedure on your team got done wrong, or skipped, under time pressure. What would it have taken for that step to happen the same way regardless of who was doing it, or how rushed they were?
  • If the one person who knows your team's messiest, most important runbook left tomorrow, what would be lost — and why hasn't it been packaged already?
  • Where do you personally draw the line between "worth writing a Skill for" and "fine as a wiki page and a Slack reminder"? Name one specific procedure that sits right on that line for you, and what would push it one way or the other.

Bring one real answer, tied to one real procedure at your job — not a general opinion about packaging knowledge.

Looking Ahead 23 — 24
looking ahead · week 4

Skills package what Claude already knows how to do. Next week: giving it access to what it doesn't.

A Skill packages a procedure — steps Claude follows using its own reasoning and whatever's already in context. But plenty of real work depends on live external systems — your ticket tracker, your deploy pipeline, your internal APIs — that Claude has no built-in way to reach at all. Week 4 covers the Model Context Protocol (MCP): how Claude connects directly to those external tools and data sources, instead of you copy-pasting their output into a prompt by hand.

This week gave Claude a memory of how to do things. Next week gives it hands that reach further than your context window ever could.

Close 24 — 24
closing · the week in one idea

Packaging, not prompting.

A grounded agent still isn't a finished one. CLAUDE.md, memory, and RAG — Week 2 — tell Claude what's true about your project. Skills tell it what to do about it: a procedure, written once as a SKILL.md, discovered by its description, and followed with judgment rather than re-explained from scratch every time it recurs. Build one badly and it either never fires or fires on the wrong task. Build one well and it's the same move as pulling a duplicated block into a well-named function — except the caller deciding when to invoke it is Claude, using judgment rather than a fixed condition, reading your description the way you'd read a docstring and adapting the steps each time rather than replaying them identically.

  • Not every recurring task is skill-shaped. It has to recur across people, be a sequence needing judgment, compress into one honest sentence, and carry a real cost if skipped.
  • The description is the entire discovery surface. Everything else in the file is invisible until that one field matches the task in front of Claude.
  • Progressive disclosure keeps the index thin. SKILL.md stays a short set of steps and pointers; the heavy material lives one hop away, loaded only when a step actually calls for it.

Grounding solved what Claude knows. This week solved what it does. Next week: what it can reach.
