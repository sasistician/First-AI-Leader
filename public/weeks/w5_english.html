Recap & Bridge · From Weeks 3-4 04 — 30
the reframe

From "it worked in the demo" to "it's ready to ship."

Every gap on the last slide has the same shape: a single agent, however capable, can't also be the independent thing checking its own work. Generation and verification are different jobs — conflating them means one blind spot does both.

That's what today's three pieces solve together — who delegates to whom, what stops bad output, and how you prove it's good enough. Starting with orchestration.

Orchestration Patterns · Part 1 05 — 30
the single-agent ceiling

One agent, one thread of reasoning, too many jobs.

Asking one agent call to generate an answer, grade its own accuracy, and decide whether it's safe to ship is asking it to grade its own homework — the same blind spot shows up at every step instead of getting caught.

  • Generation and verification are different jobs — conflating them in one agent means one blind spot covers both.
  • Independent agents can run at the same time — wall-clock speed, not just better quality.
  • A principal agent doesn't need to be smarter than its workers — just needs to know when to fan out and how to combine what comes back.
Orchestration Patterns · Part 1 06 — 30
two roles, borrowed from claude code itself

Principal agent and subagent — the roles behind every pattern.

Before the three shapes on the next slide, the two roles they're built from. A principal agent plans, delegates, and decides how to combine what comes back — it doesn't need to do the narrow work itself, just know when to hand it off. A subagent is launched by the principal for one task, with its own separate context window — it doesn't inherit the principal's full conversation, does its job, returns a result, and its context goes away.

  • This isn't a hypothetical abstraction — it's the literal mechanism Claude Code itself uses to run a subagent for a scoped task, the same one today's lab directs directly.
  • "Supervisor" (the pattern name on the next slide) and "principal agent" (the role) are the same thing — used interchangeably in this course.
  • A subagent's separate context window is a feature, not a limitation: it's exactly why a grader subagent won't inherit a generator's mistakes.

Every pattern on the next slide is built out of these two roles, combined differently.

Orchestration Patterns · Part 1 07 — 30
three shapes, one idea: delegate deliberately

Supervisor, pipeline, parallel fan-out.

Supervisor / Worker
One planner, many doers
A principal agent plans and delegates; workers execute narrow tasks; results return to the principal to combine.
Pipeline
Each stage feeds the next
A generator's output becomes a checker's input, becomes a synthesizer's input — run in sequence, not at once.
Parallel Fan-Out
Independent work, one barrier
Workers with no dependency on each other run concurrently; one synchronization point waits for all before combining.

Most real systems mix these — exactly what today's worked example does.

Orchestration Patterns · Part 1 08 — 30
not every task needs a squad

Delegate when the work is genuinely independent.

stay single-agent when

The task is small, the context is already loaded, each step depends on the last, or coordination overhead would cost more than it saves.

delegate to a squad when

Tasks don't depend on each other's output, each needs a distinct lens, and you want them to run concurrently — or independently, on purpose.

That last point matters more than it sounds: a grader that shares the generator's context can inherit its blind spots instead of catching them.

Guardrails · Part 2 11 — 30
why this needs more than "the model is usually right"

What breaks when nothing checks the output.

  • A hallucinated specific — a price, a policy detail — reads exactly as confident as a correct one. Users can't tell the difference; a guardrail has to.
  • An agent with broad tool access can take an irreversible action from a single bad instruction — the same over-broad-permissions risk from Week 4's MCP checklist, now at the orchestration layer.
  • Without a gate, "it looked right in the demo" is the only evidence you have before launch.
Guardrails · Part 2 12 — 30
least privilege, orchestration-wide

Scope what each agent — and each worker — can touch.

  • Give each worker only the tools and data its job needs — a grader doesn't need write access; a generator doesn't need to ship anything.
  • Sandbox anything that executes code or calls external tools, same trust-boundary discipline as Week 4's MCP server, now applied per-agent in a squad.
  • Log what every agent actually did — you can't audit a decision you didn't record.

Claude Code's own permission modes from Week 1 are one concrete mechanism for this — the same discipline scales from one agent to a squad of them.

Guardrails · Part 2 13 — 30
some decisions still need a person

Approval gates: where automation stops on purpose.

Not everything should be fully autonomous. Certain actions — irreversible, high-stakes, low-confidence — should pause for a person to approve before proceeding.

  • Irreversible actions — send, delete, spend — gate them regardless of confidence score.
  • Low-confidence or borderline outputs get routed to a person instead of guessed through.
  • The gate itself needs a clear owner — someone accountable for what passes through it.
Guardrails · Part 2 14 — 30
from a real project

A bank chatbot that didn't ship until it passed.

from a customer-facing chatbot for a major bank

We didn't get to launch on "it looked right." Every release had to clear a gate: pass rigorous testing against a real dataset before it reached a single customer — consistency and hallucination reduction were the whole point of the exercise, not an afterthought.

  • Several hundred real question-and-answer pairs, built specifically to test for consistency and hallucinations.
  • No ship without clearing the gate — the guardrail was procedural, not just technical.
Evaluation · Part 3 15 — 30
the same discipline from week 2, aimed at eval

The scenarios were grounded, not invented.

Those several hundred Q&A pairs weren't generic trivia — they were built from real product and policy documents, the same grounding discipline Week 2 applied to CLAUDE.md and RAG, now applied to constructing the test set itself.

  • Generic test questions catch generic bugs. Real, context-grounded scenarios catch the failures that actually happen in production.
  • This is exactly what the Scenario Generator worker does in the squad — grounded in EVBot's real docs, not invented from nothing. Today's squad starts from a smaller seed set than "several hundred"; the discipline is the same regardless of scale.

Building the eval set is context engineering. It deserves the same care as grounding the agent itself.

Evaluation · Part 3 16 — 30
not fully automated, not fully manual

LLM-as-judge first pass, human review on what's flagged.

Automated grading against the Q&A dataset caught the bulk of issues fast and cheaply. Borderline or flagged cases went to a human reviewer before anything shipped.

  • LLM-as-judge scales — hundreds of Q&A pairs graded fast and consistently, every release.
  • Human review catches what the judge is uncertain about, or wasn't specified to catch.
  • Same pattern as EVBot's own judge Skill from Week 3 — just add a human escalation path below a confidence threshold.
Evaluation · Part 3 17 — 30
the pattern, not just the story

Prove it, don't eyeball it.

  • Build a real eval dataset — grounded scenarios, not generic ones.
  • Grade automatically at scale, escalate to humans on low-confidence cases.
  • Set a numeric threshold and enforce it as a gate — not a suggestion.
  • Re-run the eval on every meaningful change, not just once before first launch.

Evaluation · Part 3 19 — 30
before you call it done

The checklist.

  • Every irreversible or high-stakes action has an approval gate, or is scoped out of autonomous reach entirely.
  • Every agent's tool access matches its job, not more.
  • A real eval dataset exists, grounded in real scenarios, with a numeric pass threshold.
  • Grading is a hybrid: automated at scale, human-reviewed on the borderline.
  • The eval re-runs on every meaningful change — not a one-time gate.
  • Someone is accountable for what the gate lets through.
